But every organization has a top tier. Ours is the Valencia peanut: sweeter, rarer and packed with more kernels per shell than any other peanut in America. It's why we chose Valencias for the Crop of Now Reserve, our small-batch peanuts roasted in Pottsville, Pennsylvania. Here's the briefing.

What are Valencia peanuts?

Valencia peanuts are one of the four market types of peanut grown in the United States, alongside Runner, Virginia and Spanish. You can spot a Valencia before you taste it:

  • Three or more kernels per shell. Most peanuts come two to a pod. Valencias usually pack three or four small kernels into one long shell.
  • Bright red skins. The thin skin around each kernel is a deep red.
  • A naturally sweet flavor. The American Peanut Council calls them "very sweet peanuts," and it's the first thing people notice.

Most U.S. Valencias grow within about 100 miles of Portales, New Mexico, where the mild, warm, dry climate and sandy soil suit them. A smaller share comes from West Texas.

Why Valencias are the upper echelon of peanuts

1. More peanut per shell

Crack a Valencia and you get a small row of kernels instead of a pair. It sounds like a small thing until you're eating them by the handful. That's why Valencias are usually roasted and sold in the shell: the shell is part of the experience.

2. Sweet without adding sugar

Valencias have a natural sweetness the other types don't. That's exactly why they're a favorite for all-natural peanut butter: you can grind just peanuts, maybe a little salt, and the jar still tastes rounded and full. The sweetness also stands up to seasoning, which matters when you're building flavors.

3. Genuinely rare

Valencias make up less than one percent of the U.S. peanut crop. Most American farmland goes to Runner peanuts, which yield more and supply the big peanut butter makers. Valencias need a particular place to thrive, and that place is a small stretch of eastern New Mexico and West Texas. You won't find them in most grocery aisles, which is a big part of why so many people have never had one.

4. Grown where peanuts are happiest

Warm, dry air and loose, sandy soil are good conditions for peanuts, which flower above the ground and then push their pods down into the soil to grow. Eastern New Mexico has both, and Valencias mature faster than other types, which suits the region's growing season.

5. Versatile in the kitchen

Valencias roast beautifully in the shell, make outstanding natural peanut butter, and the National Peanut Board calls them excellent for boiled peanuts. One peanut, three very good afternoons.

Valencia vs. Virginia peanuts

The Virginia is the peanut most people picture: big kernels, two to a shell, sold by the bag at the ballpark. It's a fine peanut, and we mean that sincerely. The Valencia is its opposite in almost every way: smaller kernels, more of them per shell, red skins and a noticeably sweeter taste. If the Virginia is built for size, the Valencia is built for flavor.

Valencia peanut FAQ

Are Valencia peanuts sweeter than regular peanuts?

Yes. Sweetness is the Valencia's signature, and it's why they're popular for roasting in the shell and for natural peanut butter.

How many peanuts are in a Valencia shell?

Usually three or more, and often four. Most other peanut types average two.

Where are Valencia peanuts grown?

Mainly in eastern New Mexico, especially around Portales, and in West Texas.

Why are Valencia peanuts hard to find?

They're less than one percent of the U.S. crop and grow in a small region, so there simply aren't many of them compared with Runner and Virginia peanuts.

Are Valencia peanuts good for peanut butter?

They're one of the best choices for all-natural peanut butter, because their sweetness carries the jar without added sugar.

Can you boil Valencia peanuts?

Yes, and they're very good boiled.
